THE HISTORY OF ROCKSTAR GAMES & GRAND THEFT AUTO: THE OUTLAWS, THE OUTRAGE & THE EMPIRE

PROLOGUE: THE EIGHT-BILLION-DOLLAR ACCIDENT

On September 17, 2013, a piece of entertainment was unleashed upon the world. 

Within twenty-four hours, it generated eight hundred million dollars in revenue. Within seventy-two hours, it crossed one billion dollars in cash sales. It did not merely break sales records; it pulverized them—surpassing the theatrical box-office openings of Avengers, Harry Potter, and Star Wars combined.

To date, that single digital title—Grand Theft Auto V—has generated over eight point five billion dollars in revenue across more than one hundred and ninety million copies sold. It is, by a massive margin, the single most profitable individual commercial entertainment product ever created in the history of human civilization.

Yet the entire global empire was born not from visionary corporate strategy, but from a catastrophic computer glitch in an unheated office in Dundee, Scotland.

In 1995, a team of twenty-something programmers was trying to build a cute, innocent driving game about police cars pulling over speeding motorists. But a bug in the artificial intelligence code caused the police cruisers to go completely psychotic. Instead of pulling the player over, the squad cars rammed the player's vehicle at full speed, repeatedly attempting to crush the driver to death.

The developers gathered around the monitor, laughing until they wept. They realized something profound: nobody wanted to be the cop. Everybody wanted to be the criminal.

This is the true, untold history of Rockstar Games. 

It is the story of two middle-class British brothers obsessed with American hip-hop and mobster cinema who weaponized controversy to build an outlaw brand. It is the story of a secret public-relations scam that tricked the British Parliament into advertising a video game for free. It is the story of an open-world revolution finished in the shadow of Ground Zero just weeks after September 11. 

And it is the tragedy of a brotherhood that conquered the entertainment world, only to tear itself apart in backroom betrayals, multimillion-dollar lawsuits, and a rogue hacker in a budget hotel room.


ACT I: THE GLITCH, THE FIXER & THE REBELLION (1995–1999)

The story does not begin in Hollywood or Manhattan. It begins in Dundee, a post-industrial Scottish city known for jute mills, shipyards, and eternal rain.

In the mid-1990s, an independent studio named DMA Design—founded by David Jones and Mike Dailly—was riding high on the unexpected global success of Lemmings, a charming puzzle game about green-haired rodents marching off cliffs. 

Seeking their next project, DMA began developing a top-down multiplayer driving game codenamed "Race'n'Chase." The concept was simple: players chose between being a police officer enforcing the law or a criminal escaping traffic stops. 

Development was an agonizing, unstable mess. The physics engine crashed constantly, and the publisher, BMG Interactive, repeatedly threatened to pull the plug. 

Then came the glitch. 

Programmer Mike Dailly was tinkering with the pathfinding algorithms of the non-player police cars. A bug in the code corrupted the spatial awareness of the cruisers, making them hyper-aggressive. The police cars ignored traffic lanes entirely, hurtling across sidewalks, smashing through fences, and relentlessly t-boning the player in a frenzy of digital destruction.

When playtesters sat down at the keyboards, they completely ignored the race checkpoints. They spent hours laughing, baiting the homicidal police cruisers, hijacking sports cars, and running over pedestrians.

The team made an executive decision that defied all commercial logic: they scrapped the police missions entirely. You would play exclusively as a ruthless criminal working for local syndicates. They renamed the project Grand Theft Auto.

Enter the brothers who would turn Scottish code into a global religion.

In London, BMG Interactive was staffed by young executives, chief among them Sam and Dan Houser. 

The Houser brothers were not typical video game geeks. They were the sons of Walter Houser, an English jazz-club owner, and Geraldine Moffat, an actress famous for her role in the gritty 1971 British gangster film Get Carter. Growing up in middle-class London, the Houser brothers were fanatically obsessed with American street culture: the raw hip-hop of Public Enemy, the cinematic violence of Martin Scorsese and Brian De Palma, and the neon aesthetic of Miami Vice.

To Sam Houser, British video games in the mid-1990s were pathetic—full of cartoon hedgehogs and whimsical fantasy elves. When Sam saw DMA Design’s rough prototype of Grand Theft Auto, his pupils dilated. It wasn't a game; it was an interactive crime film. It was punk rock in digital form.

BMG published Grand Theft Auto in the autumn of 1997. But the game had a glaring weakness: visually, it looked primitive. It was a flat, two-dimensional top-down game with pixelated cars that looked like postage stamps. How do you convince teenagers to buy a top-down pixel game in an era where 3D consoles were arriving?

Sam Houser made an audacious, scandalous gamble: he hired Max Clifford.

Max Clifford was the most notorious, Machiavellian public relations fixer in modern British history—a man who made a fortune planting scandalous tabloid exposés and protecting corrupt politicians. 

Clifford laid out a cynical strategy: we will not advertise the game as fun. We will advertise it as dangerous. We will convince the government that this game is a moral plague that will corrupt the youth of Britain.

Clifford went to work behind the scenes. He leaked sensationalized, fabricated stories to right-wing tabloids like The Daily Mail, claiming that Grand Theft Auto was an unholy "murder simulator" designed to teach children how to slaughter police officers and run over Hare Krishna worshippers for bonus points.

Conservative politicians took the bait. Members of the House of Lords stood up in Parliament, holding printed screenshots, screaming that the game should be banned before it reached store shelves. Police unions issued horrified press statements.

The result was an absolute marketing triumph. Clifford had turned a low-budget Scottish computer game into forbidden fruit without spending a single dollar on conventional television ads. British teenagers formed lines around city blocks to buy the game before the government could outlaw it. Grand Theft Auto became a massive underground hit, selling over three million copies.

In 1998, media conglomerate Take-Two Interactive acquired BMG Interactive. 

Sam Houser, Dan Houser, along with executives Terry Donovan and Jamie King, seized the opportunity. They struck a deal with Take-Two to form an autonomous publishing label based in New York City. They would run it like a rebellious independent record label. 

They named it Rockstar Games. 

Their headquarters at 622 Broadway in SoHo featured exposed brick, neon signs, and blaring rap music. They wore leather jackets, smoked in meetings, and declared war on the clean, family-friendly corporate culture of Nintendo and Sony. 

Yet Sam Houser knew that top-down pixels were a dead end. If Rockstar was going to conquer the world, they had to bring the crime into a living, breathing three-dimensional universe.


ACT II: LIBERTY CITY, GROUND ZERO & THE 3D REVOLUTION (2000–2003)

The technical leap from 2D pixels to a fully realized 3D city was widely considered mathematically impossible on the hardware of the era.

In 1999, DMA Design in Scotland was acquired and integrated into the Rockstar family, eventually renamed Rockstar North. Heading the technical operations in Edinburgh was a brilliant, iron-willed Scottish programmer named Leslie Benzies.

While the Houser brothers in Manhattan focused on narrative, music curation, and cultural satire, Leslie Benzies was the technical architect who made the impossible function in silicon. 

Benzies and his Scottish engineering team set out to construct Liberty City—a gritty, rain-slicked digital metropolis modeled after New York City. On the PlayStation 2, which possessed a microscopic thirty-two megabytes of system memory, rendering a continuous 3D world with simulated traffic, pedestrians, weather cycles, and radio stations caused the console to choke.

Benzies engineered a revolutionary digital streaming architecture. Instead of loading the entire city into memory at once, the game invisibly streamed assets off the spinning DVD disc in real-time, loading the street ahead while dumping the street behind the player.

By late summer 2001, Grand Theft Auto III was finished. It was scheduled to hit store shelves on October 2, 2001.

Then, history shattered.

On the morning of September 11, 2001, hijacked commercial airliners struck the Twin Towers of the World Trade Center. 

Rockstar’s headquarters at 622 Broadway was located less than two miles from Ground Zero. The offices shook from the impact. As the towers collapsed, blinding plumes of toxic gray ash swept up Broadway, enveloping the building. Phone lines died. The National Guard locked down lower Manhattan. Staff were evacuated through military checkpoints in the dust.

The tragedy put Rockstar into an existential crisis. 

Here they were, finishing a violent, anarchic game set in a satirical facsimile of New York City, featuring corrupt cops, car explosions, and street crime, at the exact moment their home city was burying thousands of murdered citizens.

Rumors circulated that the game would be permanently cancelled. Sam Houser refused to let the project die, but he knew they had to alter the game’s fabric out of respect for the dead.

Over three feverish weeks of emergency revisions, Rockstar made surgical adjustments. The original design of the Liberty City police cruisers—which featured the light-blue-and-white livery of the NYPD—was repainted into generic black-and-white. Flight paths of background aircraft were altered so planes would never appear close to skyscrapers. 

Most visibly, the original packaging artwork, which depicted burning buildings and explosive street combat, was discarded. In its place, Rockstar designer Anthony Macbain assembled the now-iconic multi-panel comic grid box art—a design signature the franchise still uses today.

On October 22, 2001, Grand Theft Auto III was released to the public.

The impact on global culture was seismic. 

Before GTA III, video games were linear tunnels: you moved from left to right, clearing levels. GTA III gave the player a silent avatar named Claude, handed them a baseball bat, and kicked them out into a living, breathing, unconstrained three-dimensional world. You could steal an ambulance, tune the radio to an opera station, drive down the wrong side of the freeway, or simply sit on a street corner and watch simulated pedestrians argue in the rain.

It was an unprecedented critical and commercial earthquake. GTA III became the highest-selling video game of 2001, selling over fourteen million copies and establishing the open-world template that modern gaming still imitates.

Hollywood executives watched in sheer terror as a video game eclipsed the cultural cachet of major cinema.

Capitalizing on the momentum, Rockstar immediately went into overdrive. Sam Houser wanted to capture the neon glamour, cocaine excess, and synthesizer soundtrack of 1980s Miami. 

In an astonishing technical marathon of just nine months, Rockstar North developed Grand Theft Auto: Vice City. Released in October 2002, the game starred Hollywood actor Ray Liotta as Tommy Vercetti, backed by a licensed soundtrack featuring Michael Jackson, Hall & Oates, and Iron Maiden. 

Vice City shattered its predecessor's records, cementing Rockstar as the undisputed kings of global entertainment.

Yet as their wealth and cultural footprint expanded, the Houser brothers were about to discover the terrifying cost of pushing American taboos too far.


ACT III: SAN ANDREAS & THE HOT COFFEE INQUISITION (2004–2006)

In October 2004, Rockstar unleashed their magnum opus of the 128-bit era: Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas.

It was a staggering, cinematic achievement. Moving the franchise into 1992 California against the backdrop of the Rodney King riots and the rise of West Coast hip-hop, San Andreas did not feature just one city—it rendered an entire simulated state: Los Santos, San Fierro, and Las Venturas, connected by rolling countryside, deserts, and mountain peaks. 

Players embodied Carl "CJ" Johnson, navigating gang warfare, corrupt police officers, and systemic poverty. It became the best-selling PlayStation 2 game of all time, moving over twenty-seven million copies.

Rockstar felt invincible. They had defeated critics, out-grossed Hollywood, and established themselves as untouchable cultural satirists.

Then, an obscure Dutch computer programmer found a hidden digital door.

In June 2005, a thirty-eight-year-old software engineer in the Netherlands named Patrick Wildenborg was exploring the code files of the PC version of San Andreas. Digging deep within the executable files, Wildenborg discovered unused, deactivated lines of code containing an explicit interactive sexual minigame.

During development, Rockstar programmers had built a minigame where CJ visits his in-game girlfriend's house for "coffee," allowing the player to control sexual positions using the controller. Realizing that the content would guarantee an Adults-Only rating—which would lead major retailers to ban the game—Rockstar management ordered the minigame removed.

Instead of completely deleting the raw animation assets and source code from the master disc, the developers simply erected a software flag to disable the gameplay triggers. The explicit code remained dormant on millions of retail discs.

Wildenborg wrote a simple nineteen-kilobyte script that flipped the software flag back on, unlocking the minigame. He uploaded the patch to the internet under the name "Hot Coffee."

Within forty-eight hours, the video game industry was engulfed in the largest political and legal firestorm in its history.

Mainstream television broadcasts aired footage of the minigame on national evening news. The political establishment seized upon the scandal with ferocious fury.

In Washington D.C., Senator Hillary Clinton stood before television cameras holding a copy of San Andreas, declaring that Rockstar was poisoning the moral fabric of American youth. She formally demanded that the Federal Trade Commission launch a federal investigation into Rockstar and Take-Two Interactive for consumer fraud.

The fallout was catastrophic. 

The Entertainment Software Rating Board (ESRB) immediately revoked San Andreas’s "Mature" rating and reclassified the game as "Adults Only" (AO). Across America, retail giants Walmart, Target, and Best Buy pulled millions of copies of the game from store shelves, refusing to sell AO-rated content.

Take-Two’s stock plunged. The company was forced to recall millions of discs, write off over twenty million dollars in inventory, and issue clean re-releases with the dormant code scrubbed from the silicon.

The legal wolves circled. The Federal Trade Commission launched an aggressive probe. Multiple state attorneys general filed lawsuits. Over fifty class-action shareholder lawsuits were filed against Take-Two Interactive.

The crisis metastasized into executive criminality. During the federal scrutiny, regulators uncovered a massive stock-options backdating scheme inside Take-Two. Ryan Brant, the founding CEO of Take-Two who had originally bought BMG Interactive and backed Rockstar, was criminally indicted by the SEC. Brant pleaded guilty to felony falsification of business records, was hit with millions in fines, and was permanently barred from serving as an officer of a public company.

Inside Rockstar, the psychological damage was devastating.

Sam Houser was traumatized by the scandal. He viewed the congressional hearings as a modern-day witch hunt orchestrated by hypocritical politicians who ignored Hollywood violence to scapegoat video games. Houser retreated from the public eye entirely, refusing interviews, becoming an intensely private recluse behind the walls of his SoHo studio.

The experience hardened Rockstar's cynicism. When they emerged from the bunker, they were determined to take their dark satire of the American Empire to an unprecedented level of cinematic realism.


ACT IV: NIKO BELLIC, THE CRUNCH & THE TITAN (2008–2013)

By 2006, the console market was transitioning to high-definition hardware: the PlayStation 3 and Xbox 360.

Sam and Dan Houser did not want their next game to be an arcade romp. They wanted to construct a grim, photorealistic masterpiece that laid bare the rot at the heart of the American Dream.

The result was Grand Theft Auto IV, launched in April 2008.

The protagonist was Niko Bellic—a haunted, cynical Eastern European veteran of the Yugoslav Wars who arrives in Liberty City on a dilapidated cargo ship, lured by his cousin's letters promising wealth, luxury sports cars, and supermodels. Instead, Niko finds a roach-infested apartment, crushing loan-shark debts, and a hollow society obsessed with consumer debt and vanity.

To build the HD version of Liberty City, Rockstar abandoned the stylized aesthetics of Vice City. They sent reconnaissance teams across the boroughs of New York City, taking over one hundred thousand high-resolution reference photographs of subway grates, alleyways, graffiti, and rusted fire escapes.

They integrated NaturalMotion's "Euphoria" procedural physics engine, allowing digital bodies to react organically to kinetic impacts—tripping on curbs, bracing against bullet impacts, and tumbling down staircases with bone-crunching weight.

GTA IV generated five hundred million dollars in its first week. But behind the technical achievement lay a growing corporate nightmare: the culture of "the crunch."

To deliver their increasingly colossal worlds on deadline, Rockstar studios operated under brutal, sweatshop-like working conditions. Programmers, animators, and quality-assurance testers in Scotland, England, and San Diego were pressured into mandatory eighty- to one-hundred-hour work weeks for months on end.

In early 2010, the simmering resentment exploded into public view. An open letter signed by the "Rockstar Spouse" group surfaced online, detailing the devastating human toll inside Rockstar San Diego during the development of Red Dead Redemption: severe clinical depression, decaying marriages, stress-induced physical illnesses, and management enforcing a culture of fear and mandatory overtime without compensation.

Yet inside the executive suite, the machine only accelerated. 

Sam Houser began directing the production of an entertainment undertaking so vast that it would require the synchronization of over one thousand developers across eight international studios: Grand Theft Auto V.

Released on September 17, 2013, GTA V accomplished what critics thought was impossible: it split the narrative across three distinct playable protagonists—Michael De Santa, an aging, retired bank robber trapped in a toxic suburban mansion; Franklin Clinton, an ambitious young hustler from South Los Santos; and Trevor Philips, a terrifying, unhinged, crystal-meth-dealing sociopath living in a desert trailer park.

The world of Los Santos was a technical marvel: an unbroken, breathtaking simulation of Southern California spanning sun-drenched coastal beaches, corporate skyscrapers, military installations, and wilderness canyons.

The game obliterated all historical records. Within seventy-two hours, it generated one billion dollars in sales.

Yet the real financial revolution came two weeks later with the launch of Grand Theft Auto Online.

Initially plagued by server crashes and missing features, GTA Online evolved into an infinite digital cash register. By continually updating the virtual sandbox with hypercars, military helicopters, underground bunkers, and high-stakes bank heists, Rockstar introduced "Shark Cards"—virtual microtransactions allowing players to purchase in-game currency with real-world credit cards.

GTA Online transformed Rockstar from a traditional retail game studio into a multi-billion-dollar recurring live-service juggernaut, generating over five hundred million dollars in pure profit year after year.

They had reached the absolute summit of human entertainment.

And then, the foundational pillars of the company began to break.


ACT V: THE FRACTURED BROTHERHOOD & THE HOTEL HACKER (2014–PRESENT)

The unprecedented flood of billions unleashed a bitter, internal power struggle that shattered the core trio behind the franchise.

The first casualty was Leslie Benzies—the technical wizard who had served as the president of Rockstar North and the driving producer of the 3D era.

In September 2014, following the grueling launch of GTA V, Benzies was encouraged by the Houser brothers to take an extended six-month sabbatical to recover from burnout.

When Benzies attempted to return to his office at Rockstar North in Edinburgh on April 1, 2015, he discovered that his company communications, email, and BlackBerry had been remotely severed. When he walked up to the front entrance of the studio, his electronic keycard failed to open the turnstile. Building security confronted him at the door, informed him he was no longer permitted on the premises, and ordered him to leave.

In April 2016, Benzies filed a explosive one-hundred-and-fifty-million-dollar lawsuit against Take-Two Interactive, Rockstar Games, and Sam and Dan Houser. 

The legal filing pulled back the curtain on the studio’s internal dysfunctions. Benzies alleged that the Houser brothers had orchestrated a conspiracy to force him out of the company in order to strip him of his equal share in a contractual profit-sharing royalty pool. 

The lawsuit revealed intimate, desperate emails from Sam Houser pleading with Benzies to fix broken game builds, admitting: "Please help me finish GTA V. I’m lost without you." 

Rockstar countersued, alleging Benzies had quit voluntarily and breached his employment contract. The bitter multi-year legal war ended in 2019 in a confidential, out-of-court settlement, leaving the Scottish engineering anchor permanently severed from the studio.

The second seismic rupture came from within the family itself.

In February 2020, parent company Take-Two quietly filed an official corporate disclosure with the SEC that sent shockwaves across the industry: Dan Houser—the vice president, co-founder, and head writer who had written the scripts, characters, dialogue, and satirical radio stations for every major Rockstar game for two decades—was abruptly resigning from the company.

Dan Houser walked away from the empire he had spent twenty-two years constructing. He gave no interviews. He offered no public explanation. Insiders whispered of deep creative exhaustion and philosophical rifts over the commercial direction of the studio, which was now prioritizing live-service microtransactions over single-player narrative storytelling.

Sam Houser was left standing entirely alone at the helm of an empire that had lost its technical maestro and its literary soul.

Then came the breach.

In September 2022, Rockstar suffered the most catastrophic security violation in the history of interactive entertainment.

The perpetrator was not an elite foreign espionage agency. It was Arion Kurtaj, an eighteen-year-old autistic member of the cybercriminal syndicate Lapsus$.

Remarkably, Kurtaj was already under British police bail for previous cybercrimes, housed in a budget Travelodge hotel in High Wycombe under police protection. His personal laptop had been confiscated by detectives. 

Sitting in his hotel room, using nothing more than an Amazon Fire Stick plugged into the hotel television, an unauthorized smartphone, a keyboard, and a mouse, Kurtaj infiltrated Rockstar’s internal Slack communications servers.

He posted an ultimatum in the general staff channel: "I am an attacker. Contact me on Telegram within an hour or I will leak your source code."

When Rockstar did not comply, Kurtaj uploaded ninety video files containing over an hour of early, confidential development footage of the long-awaited Grand Theft Auto VI onto a public forum.

The internet caught fire. For the first time, the public saw the unfinished bones of the most anticipated entertainment product in human history: early geometry, developer debug text, and the introduction of the franchise's first female protagonist, Lucia, set against a modern-day return to Vice City.

Rockstar released a somber statement expressing deep heartbreak that their developers' private work had been stolen and exposed. Kurtaj was subsequently tried in a UK court and sentenced to an indefinite hospital order due to his acute risk of reoffending.

Yet the leak did nothing to diminish the fever pitch.

On December 4, 2023, Rockstar officially published the first trailer for Grand Theft Auto VI. 

Within twenty-four hours, the ninety-second video accumulated over ninety-three million views on YouTube—annihilating the platform’s record for the most-viewed non-music video debut in history.


EPILOGUE: THE MIRROR OF AMERICA

From an unheated office in Dundee where an artificial intelligence glitch caused police cars to ram players into curbs, to an entertainment monolith commanding budgets exceeding one to two billion dollars.

The story of Rockstar Games is the defining cultural parable of the digital age.

They did not create Grand Theft Auto to teach morality. They created it to hold a funhouse mirror up to American civilization—its insatiable greed, its obsession with firearms, its celebrity worship, its broken institutions, and its dark comedy.

Every time politicians, moral guardians, and corporate censors tried to burn their books, Rockstar simply poured gasoline on the fire and charged the world sixty dollars for the ashes.

Today, as the world awaits the launch of Grand Theft Auto VI, the original architects who built the foundation have vanished into the shadows. The Scottish hackers are gone. The master writers are gone. The publicists are gone.

Only the machine remains—an eight-billion-dollar monument to the truth Sam Houser recognized thirty years ago:

That in the theater of modern entertainment, nobody wants to follow the rules. Everyone wants to be the outlaw.
